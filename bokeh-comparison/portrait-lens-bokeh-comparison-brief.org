* Assignment: Portrait Lens Bokeh Comparison Gallery

** Objective

Research and download photographs taken with different portrait lenses for Sony full-frame cameras. Build a local, interactive gallery that lets me compare their bokeh and rendering directly.

*The purpose is to let me judge the photographs myself*, rather than rely on reviewers' subjective assessments.

This is strictly for my personal photographic research. Nothing will be published or redistributed.

** 1. My aesthetic preferences

My gold standard is the *Sony FE 50mm f/1.2 GM*, which I own and love.

I especially appreciate:

- Creamy, dreamy, silky-smooth backgrounds
- Gentle, natural transitions into defocus
- Backgrounds that dissolve without drawing attention to themselves
- Beautifully rendered foliage and out-of-focus highlights

I dislike:

- Busy, nervous, or harsh-looking bokeh
- Swirly backgrounds
- Distracting outlines around blurred details
- Onion rings and excessive internal texture in highlights
- High-contrast patterns that remain distracting even when defocused

*Crucially, I care about the quality of the bokeh, not simply the quantity of blur.*

A lens with a maximum aperture of f/1.2 isn't necessarily more appealing to me than an f/1.4. The f/1.2 lens might, however, produce more beautiful bokeh even when stopped down to f/1.4. That's one of the main questions I want to investigate.

** 2. Lenses to investigate

Primary candidates:

1. *Sigma 85mm f/1.4 DG DN Art* (Sony E-mount). I previously owned this lens, but it was stolen. This is the benchmark that alternatives need to beat.
2. *Sigma 85mm f/1.2 DG Art* (Sony E-mount).
3. *Sony FE 85mm f/1.4 GM II*.
4. *SongRaw 85mm f/1.2*, including the Moonlit/MS version. Distinguish versions where relevant.

Additional lenses:

5. *Sony FE 85mm f/1.8*, which I currently own. Useful as a baseline for assessing whether an upgrade is worthwhile.
6. *Sony FE 50mm f/1.2 GM*, which I own and consider my ideal bokeh reference.
7. *Sony FE 135mm f/1.8 GM*, as a secondary candidate if compelling samples exist.

The original Sony 85mm f/1.4 GM and other promising portrait lenses can be included if useful evidence turns up, but don't let these distract from the main comparisons.

Verify exact lens models. In particular, don't confuse Sigma's modern DG DN Art 85mm f/1.4 with its older DG HSM Art version.

** 3. Image research priorities

*** Priority A: Same scene, same aperture, different lenses

*These are by far the most valuable images.*

Find controlled comparisons in which a reviewer photographs the same subject and background using different lenses from the same camera position, at the same aperture.

The most important comparisons are:

1. Sigma 85mm f/1.2 at *f/1.4* versus Sigma 85mm f/1.4 at *f/1.4*.
2. Sigma 85mm f/1.4 versus Sony 85mm GM II, both at *f/1.4*.
3. Sigma 85mm f/1.2 versus Sony 85mm GM II, both at *f/1.4*.
4. SongRaw 85mm f/1.2 versus either Sigma at *f/1.4*.
5. Sony 85mm f/1.8 versus premium alternatives, all at *f/1.8*.

Also collect matched comparisons at f/2 or f/2.8 where useful.

Try to keep constant:

- Camera position and perspective
- Subject and background
- Focus distance and focus placement
- Aperture
- Exposure and processing, as far as possible

Record differences in shooting conditions when known. Don't claim that a comparison isolates lens rendering if other variables changed substantially.

*** Priority B: Same scene, each lens wide open

Also collect comparisons where lenses are photographed at their maximum apertures, such as:

- Sigma f/1.2 at f/1.2 versus Sigma f/1.4 at f/1.4
- Sigma f/1.2 at f/1.2 versus Sony GM II at f/1.4

These reveal what each lens can produce at its maximum aperture.

*Keep these separate from matched-aperture comparisons.* More blur is not necessarily more pleasing bokeh, and I don't want the extra blur of f/1.2 to obscure differences in rendering quality.

*** Priority C: Individual lens samples

Where controlled comparisons aren't available, download strong individual examples from each lens.

Prioritize scenes with backgrounds that challenge a lens's bokeh:

- Trees, leaves, branches, and foliage
- Dappled sunlight through trees
- Busy or cluttered backgrounds at intermediate distances
- Small bright lights, string lights, or other specular highlights
- Complex transitions from sharp to defocused detail
- Portraits with subjects at different distances from the background

*Avoid relying on easy backgrounds*, such as plain walls or extremely distant scenery that becomes a uniform blur regardless of the lens.

For individual samples, prioritize f/1.4 when available, followed by wide-open examples and f/1.8.

Include both close-up and wider environmental portraits where possible. Bokeh can behave differently at different subject distances.

** 4. Sources and image verification

Search independent lens reviews, head-to-head tests, photographic galleries, and photographer websites.

Start with:

- Dustin Abbott
- Phillip Reeve
- The Digital Picture
- Sony Alpha Blog
- PetaPixel
- Christopher Frost
- Lenstip
- DPReview sample galleries

Search beyond these sources as well.

*Download the largest original-resolution images legitimately accessible.*

Requirements:

- Preserve the original downloaded JPEGs without resizing, recompression, sharpening, denoising, or other changes.
- Preserve embedded EXIF metadata.
- Use EXIF to verify lens and aperture where possible.
- If EXIF is missing, use explicit information from the source page and record that the information is source-reported rather than EXIF-verified.
- Never guess a lens model, aperture, or shooting condition.
- Exclude images where lens identity or aperture cannot be reasonably established.
- Record the photographer, original image URL, source page URL, and available technical details.
- Respect site access restrictions. Don't bypass authentication, paywalls, download protections, or watermarks.

If a useful photograph can't legitimately be downloaded, record a link to the original instead.

** 6. Interactive HTML gallery

This is a major part of the assignment.

Build a clean, self-contained local HTML gallery that opens directly in a browser without a web server, external dependencies, or installation.

*** A. Instant A/B toggle (primary comparison method)

For each matched comparison, show two photographs in exactly the same display area.

- Press *Space* or click an A/B button to switch instantly between photographs.
- Preload both images to avoid delays or flickering.
- Keep the displayed framing and scale aligned as closely as possible.
- Make it easy to flip rapidly back and forth while looking at the same background detail.
- Display unobtrusive labels identifying the lens and aperture.

Preserve the original files. Any alignment or cropping needed for the viewer should affect only the display, not the downloaded images.

Include an option to see the entire original frame.

*** B. Side-by-side comparison

Provide a button to switch to side-by-side mode.

Show both photographs at equal display sizes, with their lens and aperture labels.

*** C. Zoom and pan

Allow me to:

- Zoom into out-of-focus foliage, highlights, and other background details.
- View photographs at 100% pixel magnification.
- Pan around the images.
- Synchronize zoom and pan between matched photographs where possible.
- Open either original photograph at full resolution.

*** D. Blind comparison mode

Include an optional *Hide Lens Names* toggle.

This lets me judge which rendering I prefer without being influenced by brand, price, or reputation.

I should be able to reveal the lens identities afterward.

In blind mode, randomize which lens appears as A and which as B for each pair, and hide filenames and source links until I reveal.

*** E. Navigation

Organize the gallery into three main sections:

1. *Matched-aperture comparisons* (default and highest priority)
2. *Wide-open comparisons*
3. *Individual lens samples*

Within each section, group images logically by lens or comparison.

Clearly label the quality of each comparison:

- *Controlled match:* Same scene and aperture, with key conditions matched as closely as can be verified.
- *Approximate match:* Similar scene, but one or more important conditions differ or are unknown.
- *Individual sample:* No direct counterpart.

Never present unrelated photographs as a controlled A/B comparison.

The interface should be simple, fast, and optimized for evaluating images, not for decorative design.

** 7. Source documentation

Create [[file:sources.org][sources.org]] with an entry for every image.

Include:

- Local filename and comparison group
- Lens model
- Aperture
- Other available EXIF metadata
- Photographer or reviewer
- Original image URL
- Source page URL
- Whether lens/aperture information was verified by EXIF or reported by the source
- Relevant notes about camera position, background, processing, and comparability

Also link to source pages from the HTML gallery.

** 8. Quantity and quality

Aim for:

- At least *five useful same-scene comparison pairs* in total, if they exist.
- Approximately *8–10 strong individual samples per primary lens*, if worthwhile images are available.
- Multiple backgrounds and subject distances where possible.

But *quality matters much more than quantity*.

Five excellent matched comparisons are more useful than fifty unrelated photographs.

Don't pad the gallery with poor or irrelevant samples just to reach a quota. If the important matched comparisons cannot be found, say so explicitly.

** 9. Report back

Once the gallery is created:

1. Give the location of [[file:index.html][index.html]] and explain how to open it.
2. Report how many controlled, approximate, and wide-open comparison pairs were found.
3. Identify which important lens pairings have good evidence and which have none.
4. Note any images whose technical details could not be fully verified.
5. Highlight any especially useful comparisons, without telling me which lens I should prefer.

** Success criterion

I want to open the gallery, flip between images, and decide for myself:

*Which lens produces the creamiest, dreamiest, most beautiful bokeh, particularly when the background is difficult?*

I want to see whether the Sigma 85mm f/1.2, Sony GM II, SongRaw, or another lens produces rendering I find more pleasing than the Sigma 85mm f/1.4 DG DN Art I previously owned.

The goal is not to identify the sharpest or most technically advanced lens.

*The goal is to collect the strongest possible photographic evidence so I can decide which rendering I love.*

I particularly like the addition of blind A/B comparisons. You can decide which image you prefer without knowing whether it came from a $1,000 Sigma or a much more expensive lens. That's a much better way to test whether the extra money and weight actually buy you something you value.
